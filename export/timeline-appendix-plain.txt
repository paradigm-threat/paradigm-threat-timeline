N. A. Morozov

Nikolai Alexandrovich Morozov (1854–1946) was a Russian revolutionary, scientist, and polymath whose
astronomical approach to biblical and historical texts made him a foundational precursor to the modern
New Chronology developed by Anatoly Fomenko.
He spent years imprisoned in the Shlisselburg Fortress,
during which he produced much of his most significant scholarship. His work spans chemistry, physics,
astronomy, linguistics, and history.

The Revelation in Storm and Thunder (1907)

Otkrovenie v groze i bure — published shortly after his release from Shlisselburg — proposes a radical,
secular, and astronomical interpretation of the Biblical Book of Revelation.

Core thesis: Morozov argued that the Book of Revelation is not a prophecy of the end times, but rather a
symbolic record of a specific astronomical event observed by the text's author — whom he argued was
John Chrysostom.

The date: Using astronomical calculations, Morozov claimed to have identified the exact date represented
by the stellar imagery in Revelation as September 30, 395 AD (Julian calendar), based on observations of
the constellation over Patmos.

The interpretation: The vivid imagery of the apocalypse — the four horsemen, the dragon, the woman
clothed with the sun — are read as allegories for the sun, moon, planets, and constellations (Scorpio, Libra,
Leo) as they appeared during a solar eclipse on that date.

Reception: The work was considered highly controversial and was added to the index of prohibited books
by the Russian Orthodox clergy in 1908.

Significance: A pioneering work of astro-historical analysis that attempts to "demythologize" religious
texts by anchoring them in natural phenomena.

Note: The Russian title is also translated as "Revelation in Thunderstorm and Tempest" or
"The Revelation to John: An Astronomic Historical Investigation".

References:

• Wikipedia — The Revelation in Storm and Thunder

Prophets (Пророки, 1914)

A 1914 work applying Morozov's astronomical methods to analyze biblical texts, written as a continuation of
The Revelation in Storm and Thunder.

Methodology: Morozov argued that many historical events — particularly those in the Bible — were misdated
because they were based on false or misread astronomical data. He cross-checked textual descriptions against
reconstructed sky positions to identify the actual dates behind the narratives.

Significance: Prophets laid the groundwork for his comprehensive seven-volume series Khristos
(1924–1932), which detailed his revised, condensed timeline of history. While considered pseudohistory by
mainstream academics, it is foundational to the Russian New Chronology movement.

References:

• Wikipedia — Nikolai Morozov

Khristos (Христос, 1924–1932)

Khristos: istoriya chelovechestva v estestvennonauchnom osveshchenii
("Christ: History of Mankind in the Light of Natural Sciences") is Morozov's magnum opus — a seven-volume
work produced partly during his imprisonment — a comprehensive attempt to overhaul traditional historical
chronology using methods from astronomy, physics, and mathematics.

Core thesis (New Chronology): Morozov argued that the accepted chronology of ancient and medieval history
was significantly flawed. He proposed that much of early history was fabricated, with many ancient events
actually occurring much later in the Middle Ages.

Astronomical methodology: A core component is using the dates of astronomical events — eclipses,
planetary conjunctions — mentioned in ancient texts to determine the real time of the events. He analyzed
ancient religious texts, including the Book of Revelation, through a scientific, astronomical lens.

Reinterpretation of religion: Morozov argued that many biblical events and figures, including Jesus Christ,
were not historical in the traditional sense, but rather allegorical figures representing astronomical
phenomena or earlier events.

Structure: The seven-volume set covers various aspects of human history, with a focus on re-evaluating
the timeline of Mediterranean and European civilizations.

Influence: While considered pseudoscientific by mainstream academia, Khristos is significant as a
direct precursor to the modern New Chronology developed by Anatoly Fomenko.

References:

• HathiTrust — Khristos volumes

Physical Examination of Codex Sinaiticus

Morozov had the rare opportunity to personally handle Codex Sinaiticus
in the manuscript department of the Public Library (St. Petersburg), and recorded his observations in
Prophets (Пророки, 1914), pp. 258–260. His findings became a key early argument against the manuscript's
claimed 4th-century date.

On the condition of the parchment:

"Upon careful examination of the Codex Sinaiticus in the manuscript department of the Public Library, my
attention was first of all drawn to the fact that the parchment sheets of this document were not at all
frayed at the lower corners, not dirty or soiled by fingers, as should have been the case with a thousand
years of use."

— N. A. Morozov, Prophets (1914), p. 258

On the photographic reproductions (Kirsopp Lake editions):

"On the phototype images taken from it, published in England, the pages appear much dirtier than they
really are."

— Prophets, p. 259

Morozov noted this discrepancy as suspicious — suggesting the English photographic editions were deliberately
processed to give the manuscript a more convincingly "aged" appearance than the actual object warranted.

On flexibility of the parchment:

"What seems especially interesting to me is the internal condition of the parchment of the Codex
Sinaiticus. Its sheets are very thin, beautifully crafted and, what is most amazing, they have retained
their flexibility — they have not become fragile at all! And this circumstance is very important for
determining antiquity. When we deal with documents that have really lain for a millennium, even under
the best climatic conditions, then often at the slightest touch to their sheets, they break into tiny
pieces..."

— Prophets, p. 260

On the torn binding and outer sheets:

"...the condition of the internal sheets of the Codex Sinaiticus with obvious traces of careless handling
by the monks, who tore off its binding and tore off the outer sheets."

— Prophets, p. 260

Morozov observed that the binding and outermost sheets — which would normally carry the manuscript's issue
data (where, when, and by whom it was made) — were missing. He raised the possibility that the binding was
removed not by monks but by Tischendorf himself, to conceal evidence of the manuscript's actual date of
production. This was consistent with the Tischendorf acquisition story itself, which Morozov found
suspicious: Tischendorf claimed to have found sheets of the manuscript discarded in a basket intended for
burning by monks at St. Catherine's Monastery — while the surviving leaves were found to be in
exceptionally fine condition.

The 1933 sale:

After the Russian Revolution, the Soviet government sold the Codex Sinaiticus to the British Museum in 1933
for £100,000 sterling — without the Russians having publicly contested its antiquity, despite Morozov's
findings having been in print since 1914. Russian forum commentary (d3.ru, 2009) speculated this reflected a
calculated political transaction rather than genuine belief in the manuscript's authenticity.

Related observation by Porfiry Uspensky (1845):

Bishop Porfiry Uspensky, who visited St. Catherine's
Monastery in 1845 — the same year as Tischendorf's first visit — wrote that the manuscript's script was
"completely similar to Church Slavonic" (First Journey to the Sinai Monastery, Petersburg 1856, p. 226).
This observation has since been cited as corroborating Morozov's scepticism about the date.

Significance: Morozov's hands-on physical inspection is one of the earliest documented scholarly
challenges to the conventional 4th-century dating of Sinaiticus, predating modern debates by decades and
directly influencing Fomenko and Nosovsky's treatment of the manuscript in Biblical Rus' (1998).

Wal Thornhill

Wallace William "Wal" Thornhill (1942–2023) was an Australian physicist and independent researcher, and the
principal theorist of the Electric Universe
model. Born in Melbourne during the Second World War, he spent more than five decades building a coherent
alternative cosmology grounded in plasma physics, classical electromagnetism, and the cross-disciplinary
study of ancient testimony. He died on February 7, 2023, at the age of 80. The specific cause of
death was not publicly released by his family or representatives.

The Thunderbolts Project — the organization he co-founded with David Talbott
— paid tribute on his passing:

"RIP master Wal Thornhill. You opened our eyes. You have been a giant in your time.
'We need something better, the electric universe is the something better.'
Tears of sadness for your passing and of joy for your life."

Biography

Thornhill was born in Melbourne, Australia in 1942. His father was invalided during the Second World War
and the family lived modestly on a pension. An uncle — a commando veteran of New Guinea — introduced him
to the telescope and to shortwave radio, instilling a lifelong curiosity about how things worked. He taught
himself to build crystal radio sets and sold them to classmates at school.

At high school his father brought home a red-covered book from the military hospital: Worlds in
Collision by Immanuel Velikovsky (1950). Thornhill
had never seen the scientific method applied so broadly across disciplines. Worlds in Collision argued that
the ancient skies were radically different from those we see today and that electromagnetic forces dominated
the solar system during the historic period of planetary chaos. The book became the intellectual catalyst
for everything that followed.

At university, Thornhill was the only physics undergraduate haunting the anthropology library stacks,
checking whether Velikovsky had cherry-picked his sources. He concluded Velikovsky had made a genuinely
strong case that could not be dismissed. Lectures offered either hostility or evasion when questioned on
the subject. He completed his degree, began postgraduate research in upper atmosphere physics at the
University of Melbourne, then left academia to join IBM during the early Wild West years of computing —
where he developed the habit of refusing to treat anything as a black box.

In 1979, shortly before Velikovsky's death, Thornhill visited him at his home in Princeton to discuss
the electrical nature of gravity. Velikovsky gave him a slim, early manuscript he had written in the 1930s:
Cosmos without Gravitation — which Thornhill regarded as pointing toward the single deepest problem
with the mainstream account of celestial mechanics.

The Electric Universe Framework

Thornhill's core proposition, developed across decades of presentations and papers, is that electricity
and plasma are the dominant forces governing the behavior of stars, planets, comets, and large-scale
cosmic structure — not gravity acting on electrically neutral matter.

In his own words, from the Interdisciplinary Story of the EU lecture:

"The Electric Universe operates by trying to imagine what those original simple rules were, and the
result of that has been a synthesis which allows us to get a grasp on how the Universe really works in
an electrical sense. It's very simple — you could teach this to primary school students."

His framework draws directly on the Nobel Prize-winning plasma physicist
Hannes Alfvén, who warned in his 1970 Nobel acceptance
speech that an inevitable crisis in astrophysics would follow if electric circuits in space were not
recognized. Thornhill spent the next fifty years vindicated by each new crisis in mainstream cosmology.

Key principles:

•
The electric sun: The Sun is not powered by internal thermonuclear fusion but by external
electric discharge from interstellar currents — a model independently tested in the
SAFIRE plasma reactor experiment, which Thornhill considered
direct laboratory confirmation that astrophysicists do not correctly understand stellar energy.

•
Electric gravity: Gravity is not a separate, mysterious force but is an emergent electromagnetic
phenomenon arising from the charge distribution within matter. Thornhill traced this insight to his
1981 discovery of Ralph Sansbury's Journal of Classical Physics and its treatment of electron
structure — arguing that point-particle mathematics had broken physics circa 1900 and that the
classical approach was the only way back.

•
Comets are electric: Comet tails, coma, x-ray emissions, and surface features are products of
electric discharge between the comet (a negatively charged rocky body) and the solar wind, not
sublimating ice. Before the 2005 Deep Impact
mission, Thornhill published specific, testable predictions: the impact would reveal rock, not
dirty snowball — no ice. The surface material found was consistent with rocky, dry composition and
included unexpected x-ray bursts, both matching his predictions.

•
Birkeland current formation: Stars and planets do not form by gravitational collapse from
diffuse gas clouds over billions of years. From his Saturn-Earth Connection lecture:

"In our Electric Universe, stars and planets are formed at the same time inside molecular
clouds along a snaking cosmic lightning bolt."

These snaking lightning bolts are Birkeland currents,
the filamentary plasma structures that carry electric charge across cosmic distances and organize
matter into stars and planets simultaneously.

The Saturn-Earth Connection

Thornhill's most historically significant contribution — developed in collaboration with
Dave Talbott and
Dwardu Cardona — is the model of Saturn as a former
brown dwarf star that once occupied the center of a
collinear planetary configuration with Earth.

From his Saturn-Earth Connection presentation:

"The history and origins of the gas giant Saturn, and indeed the entire solar system, including our
own planet, is not what we've been told."

Key claims of this model:

• Saturn as host star: Proto-Earth was captured by Saturn when Saturn was a brown dwarf drifting
through interstellar space, outside the influence of our Sun. Saturn served as Earth's original
host star, providing light, warmth, and electromagnetic organization.

• Water from Saturn: The water now in Earth's oceans originated from Saturn's violent plasma
flare-up as the brown dwarf was captured into the Sun's orbit. Watery filaments rained onto Earth

On the JWST and Crises in Modern Cosmology

A recurring theme across Thornhill's presentations is the institutional failure of specialization:

The result is a useless reflection in a shattered mirror."

"Mathematics isn't physics."

He cited Richard Feynman on gravity — "There is no

Ralph Juergens

Ralph E. Juergens (6 May 1924 – 2 November 1979) was an American civil engineer and independent
researcher who is the originator of the Electric Sun hypothesis — the foundational claim that the Sun
is powered not by internal thermonuclear fusion but by an external cosmic electric discharge. His work
is the theoretical cornerstone from which the Electric Universe
model of Wal Thornhill and the Thunderbolts Project was built.

Juergens received a B.S. in civil engineering from
Case Western Reserve University,
worked as an editor in the publications division of
McGraw-Hill, served as an Associate Editor of
Pensée magazine, and became a Senior Editor of
Kronos journal. He retired early in 1960 and moved to
Hightstown, New Jersey — near Princeton — to pursue his independent research under the influence of
Immanuel Velikovsky.

He died in November 1979, two months after publishing his final paper, under circumstances recorded
only as "unknown causes." In 2007, Wikipedia
deleted his entry.
Apparently the establishment wants Juergens' theories to die with him.

The Velikovsky Affair

Juergens became one of Velikovsky's closest scholarly collaborators. Velikovsky recommended him to
Alfred de Grazia as science editor on a planned book
about the Velikovsky controversy. Together with de Grazia and
Livio Stecchini, Juergens contributed to a
special September 1963 issue of the
American Behavioral Scientist,
titled "The Politics of Science and Dr. Velikovsky" — a landmark multi-disciplinary challenge to
the suppression of Velikovsky's work. This was expanded into the 1966 book
The Velikovsky Affair: Scientism against Science,
containing Juergens' essays "Minds in Chaos" and "Aftermath to the Exposure."

In June 1968, Juergens co-founded the
Foundation for Studies of Modern Science (FOSMOS)
with de Grazia, Stecchini, and Richard P. Kramer,
establishing an independent institutional base for catastrophist and alternative science research
outside of academia — explicitly not a "Velikovsky foundation" but a broader clearinghouse for
heterodox scholarship.

The Electric Sun Hypothesis

In August 1972, Juergens first published his Electric Sun model in Pensée magazine:

"The modern astrophysical concept that ascribes the sun's energy to thermonuclear reactions deep
in the solar interior is contradicted by nearly every observable aspect of the sun. The
interplanetary medium suggests not only that the sun and the planets are electrically charged,
but that the sun itself is the focus of a cosmic electric discharge — the probable source of all
its radiant energy."

— Ralph Juergens, "Reconciling Celestial Mechanics and Velikovskian Catastrophism,"
Pensée Vol. 2 No. 3 (Fall 1972)

This was a direct challenge to the fusion model at the core of 20th century astrophysics. Juergens
proposed that:

• The Sun and planets are electrically charged objects immersed in an electrified galactic medium.

• Charges arise from the separation of positive ions and electrons on a galactic scale — electric
current flows from the galaxy into the solar system, powering the Sun.

• The Sun's photosphere is an "anode glow" — analogous to the luminous discharge zone of an
electric discharge tube — not a surface through which internal heat radiates outward.

• The corona is the surrounding weakly luminous plasma, not a mysterious hot shell defying
thermodynamic explanation.

Earl R. Milton, who compiled Juergens' final papers posthumously, summarized his framework:

"Juergens, however, went farther than all of his preceptors in electrifying both the cosmic
bodies and their interactions. He perceived the astronomical bodies as inherently charged objects
immersed in a universe which could be described as an electrified fabric."

— Earl R. Milton, compiler's note in Kronos Vol. VIII No. 1 (Fall 1982)

Of his precursors, Juergens acknowledged Melvin Cook
who had noted in 1958 that accreted electric charge on the Sun could account for the solar constant
without thermonuclear processes, and
Charles Bruce whose electrical discharge
model of stellar atmospheres laid important groundwork. Juergens synthesized and extended both.

Juergens also noted that Velikovsky himself never accepted the Electric Sun hypothesis — Velikovsky
considered the thermonuclear model sound despite his own work on electromagnetic forces in the solar
system. This is a significant fracture point: Juergens' ideas derived from Velikovsky's framework but
went decisively beyond what Velikovsky endorsed.

The Negative Sun Controversy

Juergens described the Sun's surface charge as negatively charged (a cathode in the cosmic
discharge). Later EU theorists — notably Wal Thornhill and
Bernard Bligh — revised this to a positively charged surface (an anode). Neither Thornhill nor
Bligh have responded directly to or cited Juergens' specific negative Sun prediction in their
published work, despite building their entire theoretical framework on his Electric Sun foundation.
This internal discrepancy has not been publicly resolved within the EU community.

Death and Redaction

Juergens died on 2 November 1979 of officially unknown causes — just two months after publishing
what would be his final two papers in Kronos Vol. IV No. 4 (Summer 1979):

• "Stellar Thermonuclear Energy: A False Trail?"

• "The Photosphere: Is It the Top or the Bottom of the Phenomenon We Call the Sun?"

Both papers represented his most mature and comprehensive articulation of the Electric Sun model.
His death came at the moment his work was at its most dangerous to the thermonuclear consensus.

His SSDI record is available at GenealogyBank.

This occurred after Wal Thornhill had explicitly credited Juergens in peer-reviewed alternative

"The Electric Universe model follows the brilliant work of Ralph Juergens in which stars are
manifestations of a cosmic glow discharge."

— Wal Thornhill, "Stars in an Electric Universe," Aeon Vol. V No. 5 (Jan 2000)

Despite this citation — establishing clear intellectual lineage from Juergens to the entire EU

Alfvén's Reference to Juergens

In a letter from Hannes Alfvén — the Nobel
Prize-winning plasma physicist who founded magnetohydrodynamics — to researcher David Talbott
(discussing the Saturn-Earth electrical connection), Alfvén wrote:

"The physical model of what you suggest escapes me. If somebody, possibly Juergens, comes with
a model, I would like to be told."

— Hannes Alfvén, letter to David Talbott (date uncertain; held in private correspondence)

Predictive Programming in Film, Television, and Games

Literature established the templates; film and games scaled them to mass audiences. The same themes — Mars as dead planet, space travel as impossible horror, instant teleportation, telepathic purges, transhumanism, zombie apocalypse — repeat across hundreds of productions with a consistency that defies coincidence. Analysed transactionally, each production carries a specific programming instruction.

This article expands on the visual media discussed in Predictive Programming: Literature as Historical Erasure. For the general overview of predictive programming, see Predictive Programming: Fiction as Control.

Mars Is Dead, Don't Go There

Since Wells inverted Mars fiction from benign to hostile, every major Mars film reinforces the message: Mars is uninhabitable, and attempting to reach it will kill you. Rocketship X-M (1950) shows a dead civilization with Geiger counters registering nuclear war — 65 years before Brandenburg published the same finding as "science." Total Recall (1990) shows humans exploding when exposed to Martian atmosphere. The Martian (2015) strands Matt Damon on a Mars so hostile that survival requires growing potatoes in human excrement — the message being that even with NASA's best technology, Mars is a death sentence. Red Planet (2000), Mission to Mars (2000), Ghosts of Mars (2001) — the same decade produced three films all reinforcing: do not go to Mars, you will die. In Spielberg's War of the Worlds (2005), Mars itself was inexplicably removed from the storyline entirely — the aliens come from underground, not from the red planet. The Deep State continues to erase the breadcrumbs that lead back to them.

Mars Needs Women

A persistent thread across Mars fiction is that Mars needs Earth's women. The 1967 film Mars Needs Women makes the subtext explicit, but the theme appears everywhere: abduction narratives, breeding programmes, the recurring motif of human females being taken. The Martian catastrophe devastated the Mars ecosystem and plummeted birth rates. Eyes Wide Shut (1999) depicts a ceremony involving naked women following orders — possibly encoding a programme that raises human females to walk through a quantum gate voluntarily. The consistency of this theme across decades of media — from pulp magazines to prestige cinema — suggests it encodes an ongoing operational reality, not a storytelling convention.

Instant Teleportation

Quantum-gate technology appears in science fiction as "instant teleportation" — always presented as impossible fantasy while being demonstrated on screen: Stargate (1994) — pyramid as portal; Doom (2005) — instantaneous quantum teleport between Earth and Mars, depicted as a literal walk-through archway; Total Recall — Mars pyramids built by "ancient aliens"; Event Horizon (1997) — a ship that folds space, arrives at a hellish dimension, and drives the crew insane (Lovecraftian cosmic horror applied to gate technology). The message is always double: the technology is shown to the audience (revelation of the method) while the fictional frame ensures no one takes it seriously.

In both, a faction that masters technology but is devoid of spirit wipes out all other factions using a weapon that targets spiritual sensitivity — telepathy or other energetic abilities. If life on Mars was once largely telepathic (as implied across science fiction), then all factions relying on telepaths took a devastating hit in the MudFlood Energetic Event and never recovered. Giovanni Schiaparelli (1835–1910)

Italian astronomer and director of the Brera Observatory in Milan. From 1877 onward, Schiaparelli mapped features on Mars he termed canali — Italian for "channels." The word was mistranslated into English as "canals," implying artificial construction. Whether Schiaparelli intended to suggest artificiality is debated, but his maps showed a geometric network of linear features connecting dark regions on the Martian surface. His observations were replicated by multiple astronomers and formed the empirical basis for Lowell's inhabited-Mars hypothesis.

Camille Flammarion (1842–1925)

France's most prominent popular astronomer. His La Planète Mars et ses conditions d'habitabilité (1892) compiled over 600 pages of Mars observations from telescopic history and argued explicitly that Mars was inhabited by intelligent beings. Flammarion was not a fringe figure — he was president of the Société Astronomique de France and one of the most widely read science writers in Europe. His synthesis represented the mainstream scientific opinion of the 1890s.

Percival Lowell (1855–1916)

American businessman turned astronomer who founded the Lowell Observatory in Flagstaff, Arizona, specifically to study Mars. Published three monographs — Mars (1895), Mars and Its Canals (1906), Mars as the Abode of Life (1908) — arguing that the canal network was artificial, built by an advanced civilization managing scarce water across a drying planet. Mainstream science spent a century ridiculing Lowell. His observatory also led to the discovery of Pluto (1930). Lowell's canal observations, while disputed, were reported independently by other observers including William Henry Pickering and Andrew Ellicott Douglass.

Alfred Russel Wallace (1823–1913)

Co-discoverer of natural selection. Wrote Is Mars Habitable? (1907) specifically to debunk Lowell's inhabited-Mars thesis. The question is not whether Wallace was right but why the co-discoverer of evolution devoted an entire book to attacking a colleague's Mars observations. Wallace was a naturalist with no expertise in planetary astronomy. The institutional urgency of his response — published the same year as Lowell's Mars as the Abode of Life — suggests coordinated opposition to the inhabited-Mars hypothesis at a level above individual scientific disagreement.

Pre-Wells Fiction Authors (1880–1897)

Percy Greg (1836–1889)

English writer. Across the Zodiac: The Story of a Wrecked Record (1880) is one of the earliest novels to describe space travel to Mars using a fictional propulsion technology ("apergy"). Greg describes Mars as inhabited by humans with an advanced civilization, political intrigue, and a dictatorial theocracy. The novel includes detailed social and political structures — not the vague "alien civilization" of later SF but specific institutions of governance and religious control. Greg died in 1889, nine years before Wells; his Mars is entirely pre-inversion.

Robert Cromie (1855–1907)

Irish journalist and novelist. A Plunge into Space (1890) describes human-like Martians living in a peaceful, utopian society. Cromie's Mars has resolved the political and social problems that Earth still struggles with. The novel was published eight years before Wells and portrays Mars contact as mutually beneficial — the opposite of the Wells template.

Gustavus W. Pope (1828–1902)

American physician and author. Journey to Mars, the Wonderful World: Its Beauty and Splendor; Its Mighty Races and Kingdoms; Its Final Doom (1894) describes human-like Martians with cities and technology. Pope is frequently cited as an influence on Edgar Rice Burroughs — his Mars features multiple races, advanced technology, and a specific social geography. He was a medical doctor, not a professional writer; his access to Mars descriptions may have come through channels unavailable to literary figures.

Kurd Lasswitz (1848–1910)

German philosopher, mathematician, and author. Auf zwei Planeten (Two Planets, 1897) — published one year before Wells — describes Martians who are superior but benign and who establish their presence on Earth specifically at the North Pole. The polar station is the point of contact between the two civilizations. Lasswitz's Martians initially establish a benevolent protectorate over Earth, though political tensions eventually arise.

Investigative significance: Lasswitz places the Mars–Earth contact point at the North Pole — the same location where multiple esoteric, mythological, and anomalous traditions locate hidden civilizations or points of interdimensional contact. This German novel predates Wells by one year and remains far less known in the Anglophone world. A full analysis of Lasswitz's sources and the North Pole connection is warranted.

Post-Wells Pulp Authors (1905–1960s)

Garrett P. Serviss (1851–1929)

American astronomer and science journalist. Edison's Conquest of Mars (1898) was written as a direct response to Wells: humans build a fleet of spaceships and invade Mars in retaliation for the Martian attack. The critical detail: Serviss's Martians are revealed to have built the Great Pyramid and the Sphinx during a previous visit to Earth.

Investigative significance: The connection between Mars and the Egyptian pyramids — which Serviss introduced in the same year Wells published War of the Worlds — appears independently in multiple later traditions (ancient astronaut theory, Hoagland's Monuments of Mars, the "face on Mars" controversy). English author. Lieut. Gullivar Jones: His Vacation (1905) describes an Earth soldier transported to Mars where he has adventures among human-like Martians. Generally recognised as a direct precursor to Burroughs's Barsoom series. Arnold was the son of Sir Edwin Arnold, author of The Light of Asia — a family with connections to Eastern religious knowledge.

Edgar Rice Burroughs (1875–1950)

Failed businessman who began writing Mars fiction in 1911 and published A Princess of Mars in 1912, followed by ten more Barsoom novels through 1943. No known intelligence connections. Burroughs had tried and failed at multiple careers — soldier, prospector, storekeeper, advertising man — before turning to fiction at age 36.

His eleven Barsoom novels contain the most detailed fictional account of Mars political structures in existence:

He is like Joseph Smith in this regard: everyone believes he made it up. The same intuition applies to J.R.R. Tolkien. Possible sources include oral traditions or suppressed documents no longer traceable; Theosophical literature about Mars and planetary hierarchies; or firsthand or secondhand disclosure that Burroughs, under financial pressure, chose to publish as entertainment.

Alexander Bogdanov (1873–1928)

Russian physician, philosopher, and senior Bolshevik who challenged Lenin on party organisation and was expelled. His novel Red Star (1908) describes a communist Mars with advanced technology, social harmony, and — critically — advanced blood science. Martians in Red Star use mutual blood transfusion as both medical treatment and a social sacrament that creates bonds between individuals.

Bogdanov later founded the Institute of Hematology in Moscow, dedicated to blood transfusion research. He died in 1928 during one of his own blood transfusion experiments — reportedly from a reaction after exchanging blood with a student who had malaria and tuberculosis.

Investigative significance: The correlation between his fictional subject (Martian blood science) and his cause of death (blood transfusion experiment) is either coincidence, a case of life imitating art, or something more sinister. Alexei Tolstoy (1883–1945)

Russian author (distant relative of Leo Tolstoy). Aelita (1923) describes a Soviet engineer who travels to Mars and discovers a dying civilization with a rigid caste system. He incites a workers' revolution against the Martian ruling class. The revolution fails.

Investigative significance: Written four years after the Russian Revolution. Tolstoy was a "Red Count" — an aristocrat who aligned with the Soviets. Stanley G. Weinbaum (1902–1935)

American science fiction author. A Martian Odyssey (1934) is considered a landmark story for portraying intelligent, friendly, non-humanoid Martians — one of the first "sympathetic alien" stories in American SF. Weinbaum died of lung cancer at age 33, just eighteen months after his first publication. His brief career produced some of the most influential alien-contact fiction in the genre.

C.S. Lewis (1898–1963)

Oxford medievalist, literary scholar, and Christian apologist. His Space Trilogy — Out of the Silent Planet (1938), Perelandra (1943), That Hideous Strength (1945) — encodes pre-Copernican planetary cosmology as fiction. Lewis's Mars (Malacandra) is inhabited by three intelligent species — hrossa, séroni, pfifltriggi — all rational, moral, and civilised under the governance of Oyarsa, a planetary angel.

Investigative significance: Lewis had deep knowledge of medieval planetary cosmology from his academic work. Leigh Brackett (1915–1978)

American author often called "the Queen of Space Opera." Her planetary romances — The Sword of Rhiannon, Shadow Over Mars, The Secret of Sinharat, and dozens of short stories — describe Mars as a dying but inhabited world with ancient human-like civilizations, lost cities, functional canals, and decadent ruling classes. Her Mars preserves the pre-Wells consensus intact: inhabited, ancient, beautiful, and decaying.

Brackett was married to Edmond Hamilton, another prolific Mars pulp writer. Ray Bradbury (1920–2012)

American author. Investigative significance: Bradbury's Mars inverts Wells's inversion — his colonisers are the villains, his Martians the victims. The Martian Chronicles encodes the destruction of an inhabited civilization by colonisers, a narrative that maps onto the proposed Mars Catastrophe read through the lens of Martian victims rather than Earth aggressors.

Isaac Asimov (1920–1992)

Russian-born American author; one of the "Big Three" SF writers. His robot fiction — I, Robot (1950), "Runaround" (1942), and the positronic robot series — became the dominant template for AI ethics in the 20th century. His Mars work is secondary but present: David Starr, Space Ranger (1952), The Martian Way (1952), "Everest" (1953 — Martians on Everest as watchers).

Robert Heinlein (1907–1988)

Philip K. Dick (1928–1982)

Investigative significance: The Total Recall premise — your real memories of Mars have been erased and replaced — is itself a meta-description of the fiction-as-erasure technique. Dick may not have had intelligence access, but he recognised the operational pattern: memory erasure, manufactured reality, forbidden knowledge buried under corporate control.

Kim Stanley Robinson (b. 1952)

American author with PhD in English. His Mars trilogy — Red Mars (1992), Green Mars (1993), Blue Mars (1996) — is the most politically sophisticated Mars-revolution narrative in the literature. The "First Hundred" colonists split into factions; Earth corporations attempt resource extraction; Mars declares independence; civil war ensues. Robinson incorporates real planetary science and a detailed political analysis of revolution, corporate control, and terraforming.

Investigative significance: Robinson's trilogy synthesises the entire prior Mars-literature tradition into a realistic political framework. The factional warfare, corporate exploitation, and independence revolution replicate the patterns laid down by Bogdanov, Tolstoy, Burroughs, and Heinlein — but updated for a post–Cold War audience. The Thunderbolts Project: A Case Study in Managed Disclosure

This article provides the detailed evidentiary record supporting the assessment in Controlled Opposition: Managed Disclosure and Gatekeeping. For the general pattern of controlled opposition, see that article.

Overview

The Thunderbolts Project (thunderbolts.info), founded by David Talbott and Wallace Thornhill, is the most prominent organisation promoting Electric Universe theory and Saturnian cosmology to the public. Their work draws from Immanuel Velikovsky's Worlds in Collision (1950) and incorporates plasma physics. For many independent researchers, the Thunderbolts Project is the gateway into catastrophist and Saturnian studies.

It is also, in the assessment of multiple independent researchers including Jno Cook, a textbook case of managed disclosure — an operation that approaches forbidden knowledge closely enough to attract genuine seekers, while systematically excluding the most significant findings.

Critically, Velikovsky himself never published his Saturn theory. He died in 1979 with the work unfinished. The Thunderbolts group positioned itself as the continuation of Velikovsky's research — but Velikovsky's own correspondence shows he was already warning Talbott that the work was going off track.

Velikovsky's Warning to Talbott (1975)

In a letter dated January 29, 1975 — preserved in the Velikovsky Archive (varchive.org, correspondence index, 1975) — Immanuel Velikovsky wrote directly to David Talbott after reading his typescript:

"Your removal of the Sun from any role in mythology goes, in my view, a little too far. It is true that in late antiquity and especially since Macrobius almost all mythology was made to represent the Sun, a vogue very much in mode at the beginning of the 19th century (Goethe's time) but also later — see my references in the first chapter of Oedipus and Akhnaton; but if there was a Day Sun besides a Night Sun, it could not escape having some role in mythology."

This is devastating in context. Velikovsky — the founding figure whose work the entire Thunderbolts project claims to continue — told Talbott directly that he was removing essential elements from the cosmological picture. Talbott's response was not to correct course but to proceed with exactly the exclusions Velikovsky warned against. The man whose unfinished work gave the project its legitimacy was being overridden while still alive.

This establishes that the controlled opposition function was present from the very inception of the project — not something that developed later.

What Thunderbolts Gets Right

The Thunderbolts group correctly identifies that Saturn was once Earth's primary celestial body, that the "Polar Configuration" of planets existed, and that plasma physics rather than gravitational mechanics explains many features of the Solar System. Their documentaries (Symbols of an Alien Sky, the Discourses on an Alien Sky series) introduce mythological analysis and plasma cosmology to a broad audience. Wal Thornhill's contributions to electric field cosmology are genuine.

What Thunderbolts Systematically Excludes

Jno Cook, author of Recovering the Lost World: A Saturnian Cosmology (2001–2026), attended multiple Electric Universe conferences (2013, 2014, 2016) and spent decades building what is arguably the most comprehensive Saturnian chronology in existence. His assessment of the Thunderbolts group is devastating:

1. Denial of post-3147 BC catastrophism. Talbott, Thornhill, and Cardona "hold that nothing ever happened after the so-called Polar Configuration came apart in 3147 BC: not with Venus in 1500 BC or with Mars after 800 BC." Cook calls this an "astounding oversight," noting that "the huge assembly of mythology which recounts these events [is] spread over three continents, with Mesoamerica detailing this with identifiable dates." The Thunderbolts timeline stops precisely where the most dangerous connections — to Biblical events, classical-era catastrophes, and the true age of civilization — would need to begin.

2. Planetary misidentifications. Talbott identified the planet between Saturn and Mars in the polar configuration as Venus. Cook determines it was Mercury, not Venus: "David Talbott had held this as a condition of the polar stack, but had identified the planet between Saturn and Mars as Venus. He has also remained unaware of the planets above Saturn." Elsewhere: "as pointed out by Talbott (who misidentifies Mercury as Venus)."

3. "Science fiction" models. Cook describes the Thunderbolts "left behind" satellite scenario as "science fiction" with "too many unsupported 'iffy' clauses." He calls the "corkscrew single entry" concept for Saturn's arrival "pure nonsense," adding that plasma interactions "cannot retard the travel of a planet. It would take the force of an electric field interaction, which is beyond the conceptual basis of the Thunderbolts group."

4. Systematic avoidance of Biblical sources. "The Saturnian people (of Thunderbolts.info) have steadfastly avoided bringing the Bible into discussion. I find this a remarkable omission." For a group claiming to study ancient mythology and its cosmological origins, excluding the single most widely distributed ancient text — which contains extensive catastrophist material — is not an omission but a policy.

5. Deletion of challenges. When challenged on his definition of the "Warrior King" archetype upon the release of Symbols of an Alien Sky, Talbott's response was not debate but erasure. As documented by forum user "grey cloud" (August 7, 2010): "You may recall I challenged Talbott on his definition of this 'archetype' when Symbols of an Alien Sky was released. His 'refutation' consisted of him deleting the entire thread."

6. Editorial gatekeeping at AEON Journal. Researcher C. Leroy Ellenberger documented how Talbott, Ev Cochrane, and Dwardu Cardona controlled the AEON journal: in the first 16 issues, these three accounted for 34% of all articles, 44% of all article pages, and 7 of the 9 longest articles. Outside contributions faced "unexplained deletions and unreasonable delays in publication." Ellenberger's invited contributions waited two years in the queue while George Talbott's attack piece against him was published immediately. When Ellenberger's memoir exceeded standard length, the editor "adamantly refused to relax the type format standards" — yet Talbott's own 47-page article received smaller type and wider margins specifically to fit.

7. Cardona's methodology. Criticism of Dwardu Cardona's published work noted: "Nowhere in the book is a single mythological tale used, let alone analysed. Instead the reader is presented with Cardona's opinion... supported by certain key words and phrases culled from everywhere and anywhere and arranged to suit Cardona's 'theory'." For a group that claims mythological analysis as its foundation, publishing works that contain no actual myth analysis is a revealing contradiction.

Controlled Opposition: Managed Disclosure and Gatekeeping

1900–2026 CE — Controlled Opposition: Managed Disclosure and Gatekeeping

"The best way to control the opposition is to lead it ourselves."
— Widely attributed to Vladimir Lenin (unverified; see below)

The Concept

Controlled opposition is a strategy in which a power structure creates, funds, or infiltrates its own opposition movements. The goal is not to suppress dissent outright — which risks creating martyrs — but to channel dissent into safe, pre-approved boundaries. Forbidden ideas are allowed to surface, but only under management. Investigators are permitted to approach the truth, but never to arrive at it.

The modern term traces to the Russian revolutionary period. Lenin's Bolsheviks were intimately familiar with the Okhrana (Tsarist secret police) method of creating controlled workers' organisations — the "police socialism" of Sergei Zubatov, in which the secret police funded and directed workers' unions to prevent genuine revolutionary organisation. The famous quote attributed to Lenin has never been located in his verified writings, but the concept was standard practice — first by the Tsarist police, then by the Bolsheviks, and subsequently by every intelligence apparatus of the 20th and 21st centuries.

The Pattern

• Attract genuine seekers. The operation publishes real, verifiable material — enough to build credibility with researchers who sense the mainstream narrative is incomplete.

• Establish authority. Key figures become the "go-to" names in their field. They give conferences, publish journals, produce documentaries.

Daniel Ellsberg (1931–2023): Full Chronology

Background and Career

• 1931: Born in Chicago to a Jewish family that had converted to Christian Science

• 1952: B.A. in Economics from Harvard, summa cum laude

• 1954–1957: U.S. Marine Corps officer, 1st Lieutenant, platoon leader and rifle company commander

• 1959: Ph.D. candidate at Harvard (completed 1962); joins RAND Corporation as strategic analyst

• 1961: Consultant to the Kennedy White House and Pentagon on nuclear war planning; given access to the Single Integrated Operational Plan (SIOP) — the U.S. blueprint for general nuclear war

• 1964: Transferred to the Pentagon as special assistant to Assistant Secretary of Defense John McNaughton

The Gulf of Tonkin (August 4, 1964)

On the night of August 4, 1964, Ellsberg was on duty in the Pentagon receiving real-time cable traffic from the USS Maddox and USS Turner Joy in the Gulf of Tonkin. The ships reported a second North Vietnamese torpedo attack. Within hours, President Johnson ordered retaliatory air strikes. On August 7, Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, authorizing unlimited military force in Southeast Asia without a formal declaration of war.

Ellsberg knew from the real-time traffic that the reports were confused and contradictory. Subsequent evidence confirmed:

• Captain John Herrick (commander of the Maddox) cabled within hours: "Review of action makes many contacts and torpedoes fired appear doubtful… Suggest complete evaluation before any further action taken."

• President Johnson told Undersecretary of State George Ball: "Hell, those dumb, stupid sailors were just shooting at flying fish!" He also said privately: "For all I know, our navy was shooting at whales out there."

• James Stockdale, pilot flying overhead during the alleged attack: "I had the best seat in the house to watch that event, and our destroyers were just shooting at phantom targets — there were no PT boats there… There was nothing there but black water and American fire power."

• Robert McNamara (Secretary of Defense), in The Fog of War (2003): "It didn't happen."

• Robert Hanyok (NSA historian), in a classified 2001 study (declassified 2005): NSA "deliberately skewed" its intelligence reports to support the conclusion that a second attack had occurred

• General Võ Nguyên Giáp (North Vietnamese commander): "Absolutely nothing" happened on August 4

The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution became the legal basis for the entire Vietnam War. Over 58,000 Americans and over 3 million Vietnamese died in a war authorized by an event that never took place.

The Pentagon Papers (1971)

From 1967, Ellsberg worked on the top-secret McNamara Study — a 7,000-page internal analysis of U.S. decision-making in Vietnam from 1945 to 1967, later known as the Pentagon Papers. The study documented systematic deception across the Truman, Eisenhower, Kennedy, and Johnson administrations.

In 1969, Ellsberg began photocopying the study with the help of his colleague Anthony Russo. In 1971, he provided copies to The New York Times, The Washington Post, and eventually 17 other newspapers. The Nixon administration obtained a federal court injunction to halt publication — the first prior restraint on a newspaper in American history — but the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 in New York Times Co. v. United States that publication could proceed.

Ellsberg was charged under the Espionage Act of 1917. The case was dismissed in 1973 when it was revealed that the Nixon administration had:

• Burglarized Lewis Fielding's (Ellsberg's psychiatrist's) office

• Wiretapped Ellsberg without court authorization

• Offered the presiding judge (William Matthew Byrne Jr.) the directorship of the FBI during trial

The burglary of Fielding's office was carried out by the White House "Plumbers" — the same unit that went on to conduct the Watergate break-in.

C.S. Lewis (1898–1963): Full Encoding Analysis

The Academic Layer: The Discarded Image (1964)

Lewis's final scholarly work, published posthumously, is a survey of the medieval "Model" — the pre-Copernican cosmological framework that governed European thought until the 17th century. Lewis describes a cosmos in which:

• Each planet is governed by an Intelligence (a conscious entity that shapes earthly affairs within its sphere)

• The planetary Intelligences are the pagan gods renamed — Jupiter is Jove, Mars is the war-god, Venus is the love-goddess, Saturn is the time-keeper

• Medieval Christian scholars accepted this architecture, disagreeing with the pagans "only in language (in voce tantum)" — calling the Intelligences "angels" rather than "gods"

• The cosmos is alive, not mechanical — the spheres produce the Music of the Spheres, and every celestial motion has meaning

Key passage: "The Pagan elements embedded in it involved a conception of God, and of man's place in the universe, which, if not in logical contradiction to Christianity, were subtly out of harmony with it."

Lewis is stating, in plain academic language, that Christianity absorbed a polytheistic planetary cosmology and relabeled it. The medieval universe was not monotheistic in structure — it was a hierarchy of planetary gods wearing Christian names. Lewis described this as a scholar; he encoded it as a novelist.

The Fictional Layer: The Space Trilogy (1938–1945)

In Out of the Silent Planet, Perelandra, and That Hideous Strength, Lewis creates a cosmos where each planet is ruled by an Oyarsa — a being who is simultaneously the pagan god, the planetary Intelligence of the medieval model, and an angel. Earth ("Thulcandra") is the "Silent Planet" because its Oyarsa fell — became the Bent One — and was quarantined from the rest of the solar system.

Key passages from That Hideous Strength:

"There is no Oyarsa in Heaven who has not got his representative on Earth. There is no Oyarsa in Heaven who has not got thousands. There was an Italian Saturn as well as a heavenly one, and a Cretan Jove as well as an Olympian. It was these earthly wraiths of the high intelligences that men met in old times when they reported that they had seen the gods."

"The word angel rather begs the question. Even in St. Paul one gets glimpses of a population that won't exactly fit into our two columns of angels and devils. And if you go back further… all the gods, elves, dwarfs, water-people, fate, longaevi."

"The gods of the heathen aren't merely devils as many of our friends would say. They are merely the celestial powers under their old, pre-Christian names."

Lewis is explicitly stating that polytheism was correct — the pre-Christian world accurately perceived multiple planetary beings governing earthly affairs — and that Christianity's great simplification was to collapse these distinct entities into a generic "angel" category. This is a radical claim disguised as fantasy fiction, published by a man known as the most effective Christian apologist of the 20th century. No one questioned it because no one expected it from him.
