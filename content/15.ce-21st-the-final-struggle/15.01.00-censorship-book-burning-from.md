# Censorship and Book Burning: From Alexandria to the Internet

> **TL;DR:** Every regime deletes the prior age’s records—fire, index, algorithm—because whoever owns memory steers the present. The same function runs from Alexandria and Qin through Inquisition lists, Soviet and Nazi fires, archive bombing, Mockingbird, and platform chokepoints; reading surviving shelves means assuming filtration, honoring gaps as data, and mirroring knowledge across traditions so it cannot die in one burn.

Every regime destroys the previous era’s records — fire, edict, index, algorithm. Whoever controls the past controls the present. The method changes; the function never varies.

## Ancient record destruction

### The Library of Alexandria

The library is usually told as one catastrophe; the durable point is that it was **hit repeatedly** across centuries by different factions, each with a motive to remove astronomical, historical, and cosmological material that could not be squared with the next orthodoxy. Mainstream dating assigns episodes such as Caesar’s siege fire (48 BCE, dockside warehouses and possible scroll depots), Christian destruction at the Serapeum (391 CE), and seizure during the Arab conquest (642 CE) with the familiar story of Caliph Omar’s order. Each successor could blame a predecessor; the outcome is the same: a concentrated loss of pre-catastrophe knowledge. Under Fomenko’s reading, several of these “separate” blows may be one event duplicated across phantom centuries.

### Qin Shi Huang (213 BCE, Scaligerian)

The first emperor of unified China ordered the burning of books outside agriculture, medicine, and divination, with special attention to histories of rival states. Resistance among scholars is said to have been punished with burial alive. The stated rationale was to stop the past from supplying precedents against the present throne.

### Roman imperial censorship

Roman power targeted texts and carriers together: Etruscan religious corpora, Druidic transmission in Gaul and Britain (the class itself destroyed), and “sibylline” prophecy cycles that the state periodically purged and rewrote so only approved oracles remained.

## Medieval and early modern

Christianisation in Europe paired conversion with **selective extinction**: monastic “preservation” often meant copying only what fit doctrine; incompatible originals were left to decay unread. Bishop Diego de Landa’s burning of Mayan codices (1562) destroyed what may have been thousands of books; only a handful survived. The stated motive was that those books held astronomy and history incompatible with a Biblical chronology.

The **Index Librorum Prohibitorum** (1559–1966) maintained a rolling list of forbidden print across Catholic Europe for more than four centuries. The Reformation and Counter-Reformation **both** burned the other side’s books, so pre-schism material passed through **double** filtration.

## Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: reframing as well as fire

Joseph Scaliger (1540–1609) and Dionysius Petavius (1583–1652) built the chronological scaffold later Western historiography inherited. Fomenko’s school treats that scaffold as **constructed**—with systematic error and duplication—then **enforced** through church and university authority. Contradictory material was not always shredded; it was often **rehoused** inside Scaligerian dates until the original sense disappeared.

Napoleon’s 1798 Egyptian campaign seized large quantities of records and objects. Decipherment after the Rosetta Stone still left **which** texts were translated, published, or left in basement storage under institutional control.

## Twentieth century: industrial scale

**Bolshevik power (from 1917)** treated history as a live edit: Tsarist and church archives destroyed or sealed, much of the pre-revolutionary intelligentsia removed (including the 1922 “Philosophers’ Steamer”), encyclopedias revised to match current doctrine, disgraced figures erased from photographs—the “memory hole” Orwell named from Soviet practice. Lenin:

> All over the world, wherever there are capitalists, freedom of the press means freedom to buy up newspapers, to buy writers, to bribe, buy and fake 'public opinion' for the benefit of the bourgeoisie.

**Nazi book burnings (May 1933)** — the *Bücherverbrennungen* — remain the iconic image: student-led fires in Berlin and elsewhere, roughly tens of thousands of volumes destroyed in Berlin alone, targeting Jewish, Marxist, pacifist, and sexological work among others. The regime also **kept** private archives of seized material; public fire and secret shelf are two faces of the same policy.

**Allied strategic bombing (1943–1945)** destroyed enormous runs of German municipal and state archives, including major library holdings; intent (purely collateral vs partly targeted) stays disputed.

**Post-1945 seizure** — American and Soviet capture of German scientific, intelligence, and historical paper — removed vast tracts from public view; much remains restricted decades later (**Operation Paperclip** sits in that harvest).

**China’s Cultural Revolution (1966–1976)** attacked temples, sites, classical texts, genealogies, and “Four Olds” (customs, culture, habits, ideas) to sever living memory of the pre-revolutionary past.

**Operation Mockingbird** (1948–1970s and aftershocks) marks a shift from **destroying** texts to **owning which books—and which headlines—reach the public**. See [Operation Mockingbird](/timeline/evt-operation-mockingbird-historical-chain).

## Twenty-first century: digital chokepoints

The early internet widened access; consolidation into a few platforms recreated bottlenecks. Algorithmic down-ranking, opaque bans, search burial, and brittle permanence (link rot, incomplete archives) perform the old job without smoke: **control which traces survive and which story reads as “normal.”**

## How to read what survived

1. **Treat every surviving corpus as filtered** — what remains is what victors or gatekeepers allowed.
2. **Treat gaps as evidence** — absence is not proof nothing existed; it may mark the cut.
3. **Mirror across traditions** — the same motif on multiple continents is harder to erase than a single library.
4. **Archive locally** — the open web is not a vault; sites vanish without warning.
