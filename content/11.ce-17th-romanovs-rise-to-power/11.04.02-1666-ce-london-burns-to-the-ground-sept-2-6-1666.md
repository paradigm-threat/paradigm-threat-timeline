# London Burns to the Ground (Sept 2-6, 1666)

> **TL;DR:** The fire ends plague primacy, erases **medieval** City fabric and records, pairs with **Cestui Que Vie** and a **grid** friendly to new finance; **City magistrates** block royal troops; **Fomenko’s** stolen-treasury metropole thesis frames the blaze as reset for **BoE** logic.

From 2 to 6 September 1666 the Great Fire of London burned through the walled city. It followed years in which bubonic plague had dominated English life; plague was finally loosening its hold on the country when the fire broke. Roughly 13,200 houses, eighty-seven parish churches, the Royal Exchange, Guildhall, and St Paul’s Cathedral—most of it medieval fabric—were destroyed; loss was estimated at about £10 million. Relations between the City and the Crown were already strained. During the English Civil War (1642–1651) London had been a centre of republican sentiment, and unrest in the early 1660s still complicated Charles II’s position. Many City magistrates had lived through the war and associated Charles I’s centralizing policies with the breakdown that followed; when the fire broke out they declined the king’s offers of troops and supplies, because admitting royal soldiers into London—even in an emergency—was widely regarded as politically hazardous. By the time Charles took a directing role from the Lord Mayor, the blaze had spread beyond ready control.

## London's Rebuilding and New Financial Order

Political aftermath is as significant as fire itself. When rebuilding plan was implemented, medieval guildhall structure and warren of organic property rights defining old City was replaced with regularized street grid — conveniently amenable to new financial architecture being planned. The Cestui Que Vie Act of 1666, passed same year as fire, created legal presumption that any person absent from estate for seven years could be declared legally dead, with estate administered by others. Long-term implications for property law, inheritance, and formation of financial trusts governing Western institutional life have never been fully traced.
