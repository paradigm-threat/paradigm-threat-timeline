## Russian Mountains (*Montagnes Russes*)

The French encountered elaborate ice slides — massive wooden-framed ramps (20–25m) coated in ice, sleds at tremendous speed. St. Petersburg had permanent installations. The French had nothing comparable. After the occupation, French engineers built wheeled versions in Paris — the ancestor of the modern [roller coaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roller_coaster). Roller coasters are still called *"Russian Mountains"* in French, Spanish, Italian. Given Hordian engineering capabilities, these installations likely existed far longer in the east.

## Napoleon's Height and Western Technology

Contemporary caricatures uniformly depict Napoleon as diminutive (~4'). Western populations bred smaller; height differential with Russian population was real. Western armies compensated with **massed, standardized artillery** (Gribeauval-era systems), **doctrine**, and — on the open record — **tethered hydrogen balloons** for reconnaissance where the [Aerostatic Corps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Aerostatic_Corps) still operated. **Plasma cannons** and **plasma-based siege weapons** are **excluded** from the Napoleonic picture: they would have exceeded what **open** 19th-century science could operationalize, and military knowledge was **already** moving behind **compartmentalized**, **denied** channels — not into mass field pieces for a revolutionary army. Balloons on the public record are a weak match for **offensive** strategic lift (slow, hard to maneuver, easily targeted); where logistics or legend imply crossing major barriers **without** dragging the full siege train over every road, this chronology prefers **classified anti-gravity or charged-mass platforms** — sturdy, stable, and less trivially shot down than a gas bag — over balloon warfare. ## Lift platforms, winter occupation, and the Kremlin

Speculative extension of the [Aftermath of 1812](/timeline/evt-1814-ce-aftermath-of-1812-russian-pursuit-to-paris) winter thesis.

### Balloons versus classified aerodynes

Hydrogen balloons in the Revolutionary and Napoleonic armies were chiefly **observation** assets: tethered or barely maneuverable envelopes. That profile is a poor fit for **decisive** battlefield lift—large fabric targets, vulnerable to shot and fire, awkward to reposition under contest. If the French (or their hidden tier) moved **heavy** capability across obstacles such as the Alps **without** matching every classical tale of cannon dragged by road and mule, the open-record balloon is not the best analog. This chronology holds that **secret military anti-gravity or dense-field lift** — hulls imagined as **sturdy and stable**, harder to negate than a balloon — is the more credible concealed complement to Gribeauval-era **open** artillery, not dirigibles-as-we-imagine-them for line combat.

### Winter: debarking and occupation

Russian winter need **not** have **grounded** such craft in the simple sense of killing lift or freezing machinery alone. The sharper bottleneck is **occupation**: holding Russia requires men on the ground—patrols, foraging, garrisons, intimidation of elites. Crews who could survive **inside** a hull may still have **feared leaving** it for temperatures and partisan country the cabin did not temper. Platforms probably were **not engineered** for months of **extreme-cold** habitation (heating, seals, logistics of life support) even if they still **flew**. **Air superiority** in that frame does not substitute for stone walls and winter stores; it cannot **govern** a city from altitude while soldiers freeze the moment they step out.

### Kremlin as winter redoubt

Scorched Moscow was not only a civilizational gesture. The **Kremlin** offered **fortified stone**, enclosure, and interior volume suited to **wintering** a large force—windbreak, defense, and a plausible path to **outlast** the season **inside the walls** rather than in open bivouac. Denying Napoleon an intact citadel and capital removed the last **lodgment** that might have turned a September entry into a spring still holding Moscow. # Napoleon invades Russia — Reverse Crusade I

This appendix extends [Napoleon invades Russia](/timeline/evt-napoleonic-wars-1812-evidence). For the stub and thesis, see the main article.

---

Napoleon's invasion of Russia in 1812 — the [Grande Armée](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grande_Arm%C3%A9e)'s catastrophic six-month campaign across the same territories that had been Moscow Tartary just forty years earlier — is one of the most consequential military events in European history. The conventional narrative presents it as Napoleon's fatal overreach: an army of 680,000 that entered Russia and returned as fewer than 100,000 survivors.

We call it **Reverse Crusade I** — the first organized pan-European invasion of Russia. Per Fomenko, the original Crusades were launched *by* the Rus-Horde against the west. 1812 reversed the direction. See [Aftermath of 1812](/timeline/evt-1814-ce-aftermath-of-1812-russian-pursuit-to-paris) for the full Reverse Crusade framework.

## The Napoleon–Britain Question

Conventional historians present Napoleon as locked in a death struggle with Britain — the great naval rival he could never defeat. But examine what Napoleon actually *did*: he sold [Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_Purchase) to Britain's own American colony in 1803, doubling the territory of the nascent United States. Britain's most important imperial offshoot received the heartland of North America — from a man supposedly at war with London. There was a major show of force across the Channel, just as in WW2, but if all of continental Europe genuinely wanted to conquer England, they could have done it, navy or no.

The question historians cannot answer: **why did Napoleon choose to invade Russia before finishing with England?** And why did Hitler make the identical choice 129 years later — turning east into Russia while Britain remained undefeated to his west? Two different leaders, two different centuries, same inexplicable strategic decision. The conventional explanation (madness, hubris, miscalculation) must be invoked *twice* for two separate men — or one recognizes the simpler answer: **neither Napoleon nor Hitler controlled their own campaigns.** Both were directed by the same financial network (centered on the City of London) whose actual target was always Russia — the last successor of the Horde. England was never the target because England housed the command structure.

## Why Moscow?

The invasion's strategic objective was forcing Czar Alexander I into negotiations by seizing the old symbolic capital. But Moscow in 1812 was no longer the center it had been. The Romanovs had relocated to St. Petersburg; Moscow was an administrative backwater. When Alexander refused to negotiate and the Russians burned Moscow themselves rather than let it serve as French winter headquarters, Napoleon had no strategic objective left. **The Kremlin** in particular would have given an occupier **walled winter quarters**; its loss with the wider burn closed off the most credible way to **hold** the capital through the cold. He occupied charred ruins for five weeks and retreated into the Russian winter.

In the NC framework, this has an additional dimension. The territories of former Moscow Tartary — Siberia, the Urals, the far east — had been incorporated into Romanov Russia only since the late 18th century. Local loyalty to the Romanov state was thin. A French-style liberation campaign promising feudal abolition might have found substantial sympathies among recently-conquered Hordian populations. The Romanov court was aware of this — explaining the ferocity of the scorched-earth response, designed to ensure Napoleon found no resources and no allies in the interior.

## The Battle of Borodino and Its Aftermath

The [Battle of Borodino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Borodino) (7 September 1812) was the largest single day's battle in the Napoleonic Wars — 70,000 casualties in one day. Neither side achieved a decisive outcome. The Russian army retreated but maintained cohesion. Napoleon occupied Moscow two weeks later. The subsequent French retreat became one of history's most devastating military disasters: cold, starvation, partisan attacks, and river crossings destroyed the army as an effective force.

## Why the Winter Was Unpredictable

The conventional explanation — that Napoleon was simply arrogant about logistics — misses two critical factors. First, the [MudFlood Energetic Event](/timeline/evt-the-mudflood-world-cataclysm) of ~1774 had destabilised Earth's climate barely forty years earlier. Weather patterns that had been reliable for centuries were now chaotic; the French high command planned based on historical data that was no longer valid. Second, whatever **edge** the French relied on — artillery tempo, supply calculus, possibly **classified lift** alongside **observation balloons** on the public record — met **logistics collapse**, **extreme cold**, and the **impossibility of occupation from the air** once crews could not safely **hold** ground or **winter in stone**. That failure is consistent with **overextension** and **climate** without **plasma** weapons (which this timeline does not assign to 1812). ## Moscow Burns: The Population Decides

When the Russians burned Moscow before Napoleon's entry, the destruction was deliberate and total. The population and military leadership chose to **destroy their own most sacred structures** — including churches they considered their holy Jerusalem — rather than see them turned into brothels or desecrated by an occupying army with no understanding of Orthodox Hordian tradition. This was not merely a military tactic. It was a civilizational statement: a population that burns its own holy city will never submit to the force that drove them to it. Napoleon occupied charred ruins for five weeks and found neither allies nor resources.

## Napoleon and the Hordian Legacy

Tchaikovsky's [1812 Overture](/timeline/evt-1880-ce-tchaikovsky-releases-1812-overture), composed in 1880, celebrates the Russian victory. It opens with the Russian Orthodox hymn *God Preserve Thy People* (associated with the old Kievan/Hordian church tradition), transitions through French and Russian military themes, and culminates in the *Solemn March* with cannon fire and church bells. The NC reading sees the last musical celebration of the Hordian legacy: Orthodox bells, Old Russian hymns, and cannon fire all belonging to the cultural sphere the Romanovs had been systematically erasing since 1613 — temporarily recovered in the national trauma of 1812.

Napoleon's defeat did not restore the old order; it accelerated the financial one. The Russian army pursued Napoleon all the way to Paris — see [Aftermath of 1812](/timeline/evt-1814-ce-aftermath-of-1812-russian-pursuit-to-paris). With Napoleon removed, the Romanovs dominated the Eurasian heartland while the City of London dominated global finance. The stage was set for the 19th century's consolidation of the British Empire and the railroad-and-debt penetration of every remaining independent territory.
