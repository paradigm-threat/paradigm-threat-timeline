# 19th Century: The Rise of Communism

The 19th century is the century in which the remnants of the old Rus-Horde finally lost their grip on Eurasia. After the crushing of Pugachev's rebellion (1773–75) and [Reverse Crusade I](/timeline/evt-napoleonic-wars-1812-evidence) (the Napoleonic wars — the first pan-European invasion of Russia), the former world-empire was carved into competing nation-states, each with a manufactured "ancient" history and a newly constructed national language. But the Romanov usurpers who held Russia still faced a problem: the population remembered. The Cossack traditions, the old communal land systems, the village communes (*obshchina*) — all of these carried the living memory of how the Horde had actually governed: collectively, with shared resources and obligations.

Into this volatile 19th-century landscape stepped a new weapon: **ideology as social engineering**. The Pyramidal Empire operators behind the fragmentation of the old Empire needed to channel the natural communal instincts of the Russian and European peoples — instincts that were a direct inheritance from the Hordian system — into a form they could control. They needed a version of communism that would not restore the old order but **destroy it utterly** before replacing it with something manageable.

This is the context in which Karl Marx appeared.

## A Note on Sources: The Most Redacted Centuries

Fomenko himself observes that primary sources from the late 18th, 19th, and 20th centuries are disproportionately compromised — hidden, altered, or removed from academic view. His own corpus draws more heavily on older documents than on modern ones. This is counterintuitive: one would expect the most recent centuries to be the best documented. The opposite is the case.

The reason is straightforward. Events like the [MudFlood](/timeline/evt-the-mudflood-world-cataclysm), the destruction of the Hordian Empire, and the wholesale rewriting of world chronology occurred within living memory of the 18th and 19th centuries. Covering up a global cataclysm that survivors could still describe required the most aggressive redaction campaign in history — far more thorough than redacting events from centuries earlier, where fewer witnesses remained. The closer the event, the harder it is to hide, and the more evidence must be destroyed.

**From this chapter onward, the timeline relies more heavily on the author's own analysis and political speculation.** This is not a weakness — it is a methodological necessity. The earlier chapters rest on Fomenko's statistical chronology, Saturnian cosmology's plasma physics, and cross-cultural mythology. Those frameworks provide the context — the causal chain — that makes it possible to interpret the heavily redacted modern record. The timeline fills in what the sources cannot, allowing us to speculate on these centuries with enough structural context to reach conclusions that can predict future events accurately. Where the reader encounters political analysis in Chapters 13–15, understand that this is the author applying the timeline's causal logic to a period where conventional sources have been systematically stripped.

## The Century of Manufactured Prophets

The 19th century was marked by an explosion of mass-published political theorists whose works were distributed at a scale and speed that no organic intellectual movement could have achieved. Marx's *Das Kapital* and *The Communist Manifesto* (co-authored with Engels) were published, translated, and disseminated across Europe with a logistical efficiency that suggests powerful backing — not the grassroots spread of dangerous ideas that the official narrative describes.

Meanwhile, genuinely independent thinkers who proposed alternative models of social organisation — models that preserved spiritual, psychological, and cultural dimensions alongside the economic — were systematically marginalised, suppressed, or simply never given access to the same publication networks.

This chapter covers Marx's manufacture as controlled-opposition prophet, the stripping of spirituality from communism into a materialist death-cult, Kinkel's superior alternative to Marxism, and the suppression of independent thinkers — the long game by which the "prophecy" of violent revolution was planted decades before 1917.
