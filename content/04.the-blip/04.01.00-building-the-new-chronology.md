# Building the New Chronology

The Scaligerian chronology was challenged over four centuries by independent scholars using different methods — astronomical dating, textual criticism, document forensics, statistical analysis, linguistic anachronism — all arriving at the same conclusion: the conventional ancient timeline is internally inconsistent, astronomically incorrect, and structurally dependent on circular reasoning. De Arcilla identified contradictions within a generation of Scaliger's 1583 *Opus*. Newton used precession to re-date the Argonaut myth 300–400 years later than conventional placement. Hardouin argued nearly all classical literature was forged by 13th–14th century monks. Morozov spent 25 years in tsarist prisons proving every ancient eclipse contradicts Scaligerian dating by 1,000–1,800 years. Kammeyer's forensic document analysis found a systematic majority of medieval charters before the 12th century are centrally coordinated forgeries.

![Building the New Chronology](/media/04.the-blip/building-new-chronology.png)

In 1973, Anatoly Fomenko (Moscow State University) noticed anomalous lunar acceleration data and began researching redacted eclipse dating. By 1981, the [New Chronology](https://chronologia.org/en/seven/history.html) was underway, built on three quantitative methods: frequency analysis of proper names (duplicate distributions indicate duplicate chronicles), numerical dynasty encoding (identical reign-length vectors indicate copying), and astronomical eclipse verification (back-calculated eclipses consistently fall in the medieval period, not classical antiquity). Fomenko discovered three chronological shifts — roughly 333, 1,053, and 1,800 years — showing the Scaligerian textbook was compiled from four copies of one brief chronicle. The 1,053-year shift is directly relevant to the Blip. Eight scholars, four centuries, four countries, five methods — all pointing to the same conclusion: **the Scaligerian first-millennium CE timeline is constructed chronology assembled from displaced copies of medieval sources**.
