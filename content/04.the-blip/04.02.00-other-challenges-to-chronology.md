# Other Challenges to Chronology

Multiple independent researchers concluded the Scaligerian timeline contains a substantial phantom gap, disagreeing on size but converging on structure: Illig identifies ~297 phantom years (614–911 CE) from calendar drift and absent archaeology; Heinsohn finds ~700 years of "ghost empires" via stratigraphy; Velikovsky shifts Egyptian dates ~500 years through comparative textual analysis; Fomenko's statistical dynasty analysis yields ~1,053 phantom years in the first millennium CE. All agree something is missing — archaeological, stratigraphic, astronomical, and statistical evidence point to phantom centuries concentrated between late antiquity and the High Middle Ages, when the Church controlled scriptoria. The mechanism is institutional fabrication by those controlling record-keeping.

![Other Challenges to Chronology](/media/04.the-blip/other-challenges-to-chronology.png)

This project adopts the largest gap: **1,723 years** (670 BCE → 1053 CE), combining Fomenko's statistical evidence with Jno Cook's orbital chronology. The estimates nest — Illig's 297 is a subset of Fomenko's 1,053; this project extends Fomenko by adding ~670 years from Cook. A key confirmation: the Julian calendar gains ~1 day every 128 years, so 1,582 years since adoption (46 BCE) should produce ~12.4 days of drift — but Gregory XIII's 1582 reform corrected only 10 days, leaving ~2.4 missing days ≈ ~307 phantom years, close to Illig's figure. Fomenko absorbs this into his larger shift: the calendar was not running because those years did not happen. Gap size determines how recently cataclysm ended, how much living memory survived, and how much redaction was needed.
