# Authors Who Read Ancient Sources as World Cataclysm

A large and diverse body of authors argues that ancient myth, scripture, and iconography are not primitive superstition or allegory but **eyewitness records of real planetary and crustal catastrophes** within the last several thousand years. They treat the sources as data: something actually happened in the sky and on the earth, and the stories and symbols encode it.

Velikovsky (*Worlds in Collision*, *Ages in Chaos*) reopened catastrophism for a mass audience and tied myth to close approaches of Venus and Mars — but in his published work he **omitted Saturn** as the dominant ancient sun, despite having identified it in private. De Santillana and von Dechend (*Hamlet's Mill*) showed that myth worldwide preserves precise astronomical and precessional lore, often in coded form, and that a "great year" and lost cosmology underpin it. The Saturnian school — Talbott, Cardona, Jno Cook, and others — restored Saturn to the centre of the picture: the polar configuration, the Golden Age, and the catastrophic transition to the present order. Thornhill and the Electric Universe (EU) side add plasma physics and discharge phenomena; Peratt supplied laboratory and petroglyph evidence for ancient plasma instabilities. Schoch, Hancock, Heinsohn, and many others challenge mainstream chronology, archaeology, or both, and read ancient texts and monuments as evidence of lost civilisations and repeated cataclysms.

These authors disagree on dates, mechanisms, and the exact sequence of events. What they share is the premise that the past was far more violent and cosmically strange than textbooks allow, and that the ancients were reporting what they saw. For the timeline's purposes they are essential: they provide the interpretive frame (catastrophist, Saturnian, EU, alternative chronology) without which the events in this project would have no coherent tradition to build on.

**Controlled opposition and divide-and-conquer.** The author's thesis is that the public record of Saturnian cosmology has been shaped by a deliberate strategy: Velikovsky opened the door to catastrophism but left Saturn out; Talbott and the Thunderbolts project put Saturn back in but imposed a **Golden-Age-only boundary**, forbidding or marginalising discussion of what happened after — a "control position" that can be traced in Jno Cook's writings on Thunderbolts. The result is a **divide-and-conquer** dynamic: Velikovskians vs. Talbott camp vs. mainstream, with the public dismissing both heterodox sides. Talbott and many of his associates, in this reading, function as **controlled opposition** — unscientific, flawed theories (e.g. untenable collinear gravity configurations), straw men that delay serious uptake — while Wallace Thornhill, despite the association, appears to be the real deal.
