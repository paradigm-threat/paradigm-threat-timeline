## Fomenko: the two branches

[Fomenko's chronologia](https://chronologia.org/en/how_it_was/02_06.html) describes two Christianities after the crucifixion. **Royal (Ancestral)** Christianity is the religion of Christ's relatives and the imperial clan — they honor Christ as God but treat their own blood as sacred too, like a family of quarrelling gods (Egyptian and Greek god-king parallels). **Apostolic** Christianity is disciples and commoners: no royal descent, refusal to worship emperors as gods, and in his telling many early martyrs. The later church's claim to be "Apostolic" only makes sense if another Christianity once existed beside it.

He traces a long split: royal strains toward polytheistic / Gnostic forms; apostolic strains toward Judaism, Catholicism, and Islam as we read them now. Circumcision and Paul's war on the Judaizers sit in that fight — one side forcing old covenant marks on converts, Paul calling that slavery again (Galatians). His reconstruction of this drama is anchored in the High Middle Ages forward; it does not reconstruct how long the Istanbul temple church had already operated before Christ appeared.

## Where the vocabulary and the institution diverge

Elsewhere this chronology treats the church in Istanbul as ancient — long predating the 12th-century Christ. If the institution survived centuries before Christ, its rites are not invented by the disciples; they continue older forms. The same chronology reads their function across the Dark Ages as control of food and money, bondage of people, and mediation of an invisible creator — the pattern the Deep State holy empire elsewhere describes. Christ threatened **that ledger**, not only a doctrinal dispute.

**Royal Christianity** names the movement around the historical figure: popular, not palace bloodline. People honored him as king because he spoke for them; Davidic genealogy fits better as a later scriptural retrofit (and met longer resistance in Rus). The institution that branded itself Apostolic was already adversary at his arrival, not a persecuted flock of his invention. After the crucifixion, roughly two centuries pass in which the establishment can act as if he had not mattered; then he is folded in — next savior and Son of God under the same invisible-deity frame, with celestial emphasis shifting (Sun, Mars) as astrology required. The name Apostolic works as cover for continuity with the pre-Christ cult.

## Who suffered — and who were not martyrs

The textbook picture — apostles crushed by "royal" power — fails if Royal is taken here as the people's Christianity (king in spirit, not dynasty). The bloodiest clash is Crusader revenge for the crucifixion against the circumcision party, Istanbul temple apparatus, and Judaizers — not "Christ's royal kin vs humble apostolic masses." See [First Crusade and the Trojan War](/timeline/evt-the-first-crusade-trojan-war) and [The Brother on the Cross](/timeline/evt-the-brother-on-the-cross).

Church and Fomenko often cast the early apostolic side as martyrs. For the First Crusade layer, that label does not hold: those on the losing side understood guilt around the crucifixion and faced something close to peasant or mob justice — repentance was common; some were killed anyway. That is punishment, not martyrdom. Martyrdom would require dying for the justice of the cross — insisting Christ deserved execution. No record of such a stance among those swept up in revenge has been surfaced. Without it, martyr is the wrong word.
