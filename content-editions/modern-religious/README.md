# Modern religious (interfaith) edition

This tree is for readers who practice a living religion — Muslim, Hindu, Christian, Jewish, Buddhist, Sikh, Jain, Zoroastrian, Indigenous, and others — and who want one coherent alternate history that respects their existing faith without asking them to convert, rank God(s), or swap creeds.

**Method: interpretation only.** The book proposes how overlapping myths, calendars, catastrophes, and archive politics might fit together if timelines were compressed or duplicated and records were curated. Evidence is historical and comparative: parallel stories, parallel dates, parallel suspicions about who kept the books.

**Chronology:** This edition follows the project’s **New Chronology (NC)** reconstruction — the same Fomenko-group spine as definitive `content/` (duplicated timelines, Horde/Rus identity threads, revised medieval dating). Religious prose does not re-prove NC; it interprets its religious implications for interfaith readers.

**Editorial source:** adapt from `content/` (definitive / default edition) as the technical spine; align voice with **docs/BOOK_EDITIONS_PLAN.md** (religious edition: interpretation covenant and shared narrative policies). New chapters: mirror paths under this tree, then apply interfaith passes (rotate examples, avoid supersessionism).

## Perspective in this edition — physics, evil, and power

Good and evil before God and devil. In running prose we treat good and evil as the older, broader abstractions — hard to prove as cosmic facts, unavoidable as moral judgments people and communities actually make. The familiar pairing God and the Devil is read here as language that grew out of that good–evil frame, not as the thing we settle first. We do not argue whether gods, devils, or angels exist, or what heaven and hell really are; readers keep their metaphysics. We do argue that some patterns in history and behavior can be weighed, compared, and in part quantified — dates, casualties, institutional choices, who benefited, what was taught.

No cabal, no conspiracy masterplot. The same pressures the definitive timeline describes — archives, courts, empire, ideology — appear here as human incentives and habits, not as a single hidden room running the world. We avoid the centralized deep-state story; we also avoid a conspiracy tone. Harm is traced to good and evil as capacities in everyone: envy, courage, discipline, cowardice, nurture, teaching, and neglect — not to a cartoon devil pulling strings.

Religious identity ≠ goodness. We highlight wars inside the same named religion — Christian against Christian, Muslim against Muslim — as proof that labels and sincerity are not the same as virtue. Good and evil are treated as patterns that spread socially (what communities reward, punish, and repeat); they have more to do with how people are raised and taught than with how they are born.

Prester John, Rus–Horde / Tartary, and presbyter leadership. Medieval Europe circulated tales of **Prester John** — a wealthy Christian king in the East whose alliance Western rulers sought (envoys, appeals for aid against common threats). In the NC reading used here (Fomenko et al.), that legend is not random myth: it is a distorted, fragmented memory of the Rus–Horde / “Tartarian” imperial sphere — the Christian-recognized power in the East that Latin chronicles later split, mocked, or relabeled. Canonical chapters identify Prester John with figures such as Ivan Kalita / Batu and treat Rus as the “kingdom of Prester John”; eastern warriors bearing the cross are part of the same picture. **Original Christianity** in this reconstruction is the pre-schism, pre-erasure practice anchored in that world — later fractured by schism, inquisition, Romanov-era rewrite, and competing imperial archives, until the unified horizon became the competing brands we inherit. When this edition speaks of leadership in peaceable, communal interpretation, it traces that role — etymologically and narratively — to the presbyter idea (Greek *presbyteros*, elder) and the medieval Prester title, not to a claim that any single modern denomination is the unchanged institutional continuation of the Horde church. Presbyterian (and similar) names in later history can be read as distant echoes of that eastern presbyter / Prester line once Western Europe misnamed and then forgot what it was calling Prester John.

Monotheism and the First Commandment. One thread in this edition: exclusive monotheism as a later hero-worship and misunderstanding of texts — especially a rigid reading of *no other gods before me* as meaning (a) no other powers were ever acknowledged and (b) it is forbidden even to discuss them. Another reading is available: priority and loyalty, not a denial that other nations named and feared lords of heaven in their own languages. That opens space for the thesis that communities once lived closer to a shared religious imagination, with interpretation led by elders in the Prester John / Rus–Horde horizon — more peace than the sword — before that unity was edited out of the standard story.

Where “evil” comes from in this telling. Not from a personified devil as history’s engineer, but from the unvirtuous, undisciplined, and unaccountable — and from institutions when they break trust. Fear of God (however your tradition names the sacred) is described as something that often restrained ordinary cruelty when communal faith was strong; religious warfare, corrosion of trust, and ideologies that dismiss transcendence and discipline weakened that brake. The fracturing of Western Christianity and the spread of worldviews that treat order as only what force builds from chaos are presented as major factors in Christians killing Christians, in widening gaps between peoples who once shared more, and in the soil from which later horrors — corrupt aristocracy, expansionist ideology, genocide, eugenics, and the rest — could grow. These claims are argued with sources and dates, not as sectarian score-settling against any one pew today.

Physics. Full technical argument stays on the definitive timeline and repository. Here, sky, catastrophe, and “natural philosophy” appear as witness and pattern in human story — aligned with this moral-historical lens, not as a separate metaphysical debate with your faith.
