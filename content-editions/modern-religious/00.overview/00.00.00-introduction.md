History as usually taught often looks like a tumbleweed — detail piled on detail until no thread leads cleanly back to a root. It can also be read as a tree: a trunk, branches, a shape an ordinary reader can follow. The tumbleweed form is what memory looks like after long ages of selection, loss, and re-copying by whoever held scriptoria, courts, temples, monasteries, and presses. That tilt is better read as pattern and incentive than as one tidy conspiracy: many hands, many fears, many wins and losses — not proof that every guess is true, but proof that **narrative is never innocent**.

This edition is written for religious people — of any lineage that still tells a sacred story about the world. You are not asked to leave your community, deny your prophets or founders, or accept a new creed in place of your own. We are not here to settle whether gods, devils, and angels exist, or what heaven and hell finally are. Those questions belong to you, your teachers, and your texts. **We are here to read history** in a way that still lets good and evil — older, rougher words — do their work as judgments people actually make, while some things (dates, chains of office, body counts, who signed what) can be lined up and compared.

The spine is **New Chronology (NC)** — the same Fomenko-group reconstruction as the definitive timeline (duplicated / compressed chronicles, Rus–Horde and “Tartarian” imperial memory). This introduction does not reprove every NC step; it interprets what that reconstruction suggests for faith, unity, and loss.

## Good, evil, God, and devil

Before the familiar poles of God and the Devil hardened in popular imagination, communities already lived inside good and evil as abstractions — never fully provable as cosmic machinery, impossible to escape as how we praise and blame. In this book, God–devil language is treated as something that grew from that deeper pair, not as the first move in every argument. Physics and sky history stay light on purpose: the full technical case lives on the definitive timeline; here they appear as witness in the human story, not as a rival catechism to your faith.

Evil, in our telling, is not mainly a horned plotter behind the curtain. It is what unvirtuous, undisciplined, and unaccountable human action does — alone and in crowds — when restraint fails. Good spreads the same way: nurture, teaching, habit, mercy. We treat both as **patterns that move through cultures** more than as stains fixed at birth.

## No cabal — still the same pressures

We describe many of the same influences the wider project tracks: who kept archives, who won wars, what was taught in schools and pulpits, which ideas traveled with empire. We do not frame that as a single centralized cabal or a conspiracy that explains everything. Negative outcomes are credited to real incentives, broken trust, ideology, and ordinary human good and evil — the kinds every tradition has tried to name and tame.

That matters for fairness to believers: ordinary people in the pews, mosques, and temples are not treated as foot soldiers of a hidden master plan. When institutions fail, we name offices and choices where the record allows — without turning history into a ghost story.

## When the faithful fight the faithful

One line we will not soft-pedal: claiming religion has never guaranteed goodness. The same scriptures and slogans have stood on both sides of cruelty. Christian armies and polities have killed Christians; Muslim powers have warred with Muslims; the pattern repeats wherever a name becomes a flag. That is not an attack on your sincerity; it is evidence that **virtue is practiced**, not inherited with a label — and that interpretation and discipline matter as much as affiliation.

## Monotheism, commandment, and a wider unity

We suggest, carefully and with sources, that hard exclusive monotheism — read as “there are no other powers worth naming” — sometimes owes more to later hero-worship and fear than to the oldest layers of text. The First Commandment’s *no other gods before me* can be heard as priority and loyalty — who comes first in covenant — without forcing every reader to deny that other peoples named heavens and powers in their own languages. Many monotheists have read it the stricter way; we argue that reading is not the only one the words require.

## Prester John, the Rus–Horde / Tartarian horizon, and presbyter leadership

For centuries, Western Europe told stories of **Prester John** — a Christian king in the East, fabulously rich, sometimes sought as ally (envoys, pleas for aid when the Ottomans pressed Christendom). That legend is usually filed under curiosity. On this timeline it is read, following Fomenko and the NC sources the repo cites, as scraps of real memory: Prester John names the Christian-recognized power of the Rus–Horde / “Tartarian” sphere — the eastern empire later Latin and colonial narrative rebranded as alien Tartar or Mongol when the center had to be forgotten or demonized. Canonical chapters identify the figure with rulers such as Ivan Kalita / Batu and treat Rus as the kingdom those tales meant; warriors under the cross belong to the same picture before the record was split into incompatible stories.

**Original Christianity**, in this reconstruction, is that world before the worst schisms, inquisitions, Romanov rewriting of Russian history, and the long ridicule of anyone who pointed to Prester John or Tartary. It is not a dig at your parish today; it is a geographic and chronological claim about where unified practice last had an imperial roof — and how competing archives and wars erased the view from the West.

Presbyter (Greek *presbyteros*, elder) and the medieval title Prester share a leadership image: teachers and elders, not a trademark held by one modern party. When we trace peaceable interpretation to that East, we mean the etymological and narrative line from Prester John to presbyter governance — not that every Presbyterian body today is literally the Horde church unchanged. Later names can still be read as distant echoes of a center Europe misnamed, then lost. ## Fear of God, fracture, and the modern break

Where faith was strong, fear of God — awe and moral consequence, in whatever vocabulary your tradition uses — often checked the worst impulses of the chaotic and the proud. Religious warfare, diminishing trust, and teaching that sacred order no longer binds wore that check thin. We trace how breaks inside Western Christianity and the rise of worldviews that dismiss transcendence, fixed law, and spiritual discipline — treating order as only what power extracts from chaos — widened the gaps between peoples who once shared more. Those gaps became breeding grounds for endless war, corrupt aristocracy, expansionist myth, genocide, eugenics, and the other documented horrors of recent centuries — not as a single ideology’s fault alone, but as fruit of a long unraveling.

The compiler’s original introduction described a Jewish upbringing, difficulty squaring received narrative with evidence, and a turn toward software where logic seemed to close. The same arc — trust eroded by captured platforms, surveillance, artificial scarcity — rhymes with history: wherever a tool might spread knowledge, incentives appeared to pull it back. Medicine, energy, education, and agriculture show the same silhouette.

## Interpretation, not conversion

The solution offered here still lies in interpretation: how your texts, your calendar, your rituals, and your sense of the sacred can absorb an alternate chronology without a leap into unbelief or a flattening syncretism. Another tradition’s names for powers are treated as their languages for real experience — not as a vote on ultimate Reality in your theology. Ontology stays with you; history is what we compare.

When flood language echoes from Mesopotamia to India, when lawgivers and sky powers recur under different names, when holy books disagree on dates yet agree that something enormous happened in deep time, this chronology treats that convergence as evidence — not shallow “copying,” but a possible backbone of memory later edited into separate authorized versions.

## The Paradigm Threat

If victors write history, centuries of war leave a thin, polished public layer. Causality still matters. Using OCR, cross-language comparison, and sourced argument, this timeline assembles one alternate reconstruction for those willing to weigh it.

Around 2010, many unorthodox topics flourished briefly on the open web, then vanished in coordinated waves — bans, demonetizing, ranking tricks. Materials were archived before loss and later published as the Paradigm Threat Repository. Heavy suppression is a signal: not that every suppressed claim is true, but that who controls visibility is a historical fact worth weighing — without inventing a single puppet-master to explain it.

The wider project poses an intellectual challenge to settled stories in history, cosmology, medicine, physics, religion, finance, and governance — with citations and cross-references. **This edition is science-light:** celestial drama appears as witness and pattern; full technical argument stays on the canonical timeline and repository for readers who want it.

## Heavenly powers in the chapters ahead

Sacred texts and myths speak of lords of heaven, hosts, idols, and national gods. In this book those names are read as what nations experienced and worshiped — real in cult and terror — while you decide, within your own tradition, how that maps onto ultimate Reality. Where chronology is disputed, your creed need not be at stake; where records were edited, lay believers were often the last to know.

**If a paradigm cannot survive scrutiny, it should not command uncritical assent.** The invitation is look — together, without surrendering what you hold sacred, and without pretending that pious language ever absolved evil done in its name.
